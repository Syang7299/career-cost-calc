Master Electrician License: Requirements, Cost & Is the Wait Worth It?

The license fees are almost an afterthought - $125 to $470 total. The real cost is years of your career, and depending on which state you're in, that wait is more than 3 times longer than it needs to be.

CareerCost Guides · Updated September 2026

Our electrician calculator covers the apprenticeship-to-journeyman path. Master is the next tier up, and it's a genuinely different kind of cost - almost entirely time, not money - which makes it a very different planning problem than anything our calculator's cost fields capture.

The required wait varies 3.5x by state

Every state requires additional supervised experience as a journeyman before you can even sit for the master exam - on top of the roughly 4 years and 8,000 hours it already took to become a journeyman. How much additional time depends entirely on where you're licensed, per a state-by-state breakdown of master electrician requirements:

Years required as a journeyman before the master exam

Idaho and Texas require the least additional time; Maryland requires the most. Most states fall somewhere in between.

Texas specifically requires 12,000 total career hours as an electrician, with a minimum of 2 years holding the journeyman license - matching what our Texas electrician guide already covers. Some states offer a partial shortcut: Alabama credits up to 2 years of approved electrical education toward the requirement, and engineering graduates in Colorado can qualify with minimal additional experience.

That's it - a few hundred dollars, not a major financial commitment. The exam itself is more comprehensive than the journeyman exam, typically covering the National Electrical Code at a deeper level, load calculations, and often basic business law, since a master license is what lets you eventually hold an electrical contractor business license.

What a master license actually unlocks

The wage bump isn't the only reason people pursue it. A master license is typically the prerequisite for holding an electrical contractor business license - the credential that lets you legally run your own crew, pull permits under your own name, and bid jobs as a business rather than an employee. Journeymen can usually work independently on smaller tasks depending on the state, but taking on apprentices, supervising a crew, and operating as a contractor generally requires master-level licensure first. If your goal is eventually owning an electrical business rather than staying an employee long-term, the master license isn't optional - it's the gate you have to go through either way, which changes how you should weigh the multi-year wait.

The real payback question isn't the fee

Master electricians typically earn 20-35% more per hour than journeymen - roughly $8-$20+ more per hour, which works out to an additional $16,000-$40,000 a year at a standard 2,000-hour work year. Against a $125-$470 fee, that wage gain pays back the license cost almost immediately once you're licensed. The actual decision isn't "can I afford the license" - it's whether waiting 2-7 extra years (depending on your state) for that wage bump is worth it compared to other ways you could spend that time, like starting your own contracting business as a journeyman in a state that allows it.

Before you plan around a timeline: confirm your specific state's exact hour and year requirements directly with its licensing board - they range from Idaho's roughly 2 years to Maryland's roughly 7, and using the wrong state's number will throw off your planning by years, not months.

FAQ

How long does it take to become a master electrician?

It varies enormously by state, on top of the roughly 4 years it already takes to become a journeyman. Idaho and Texas require as little as 2 additional years as a journeyman. Hawaii requires 5 years. Delaware requires 6. Maryland requires up to 7 years of supervised experience before you're even eligible to sit for the master exam - more than 3 times longer than the fastest states.

How much does a master electrician license cost?

The fees themselves are small: exam fees typically run $75-$300 and license fees run roughly $45-$170, for a total of about $125-$470 depending on the state. That's a fraction of what most people assume, because the real cost of a master license isn't money - it's the years of required experience beforehand.

How much more do master electricians make than journeymen?

The master premium typically runs 20-35% higher hourly pay - roughly $8-$20+ more per hour than journeyman wage. Over a standard 2,000-hour work year, that's an additional $16,000-$40,000 in gross income, which means the license fees themselves pay back almost immediately once you're licensed - the real decision is whether the multi-year wait is worth it.
