Personal Trainer Business Startup Cost: LLC & Equipment

Certification gets you licensed to train. It doesn't get you a business entity or anything to train clients with. Here's what forming an LLC and buying real equipment actually costs, separate from certification and insurance.

CareerCost Guides · Updated September 2026

Our personal trainer calculator covers certification, and our insurance guide covers liability coverage. This guide covers the two costs left out of both: forming a business entity and buying equipment.

Forming an LLC

LLC formation isn't required to work as a trainer, but many trainers choose to for liability separation. Per a state-by-state LLC filing fee breakdown, state filing fees average $123, plus roughly $73 in first-year annual/registered-agent fees - about $224 total in year one. The range is wide: Montana charges as little as $35, while Massachusetts charges up to $500. Most states fall in the $50-$200 range.

What's not included in these numbers

This guide covers formation and equipment specifically - a few real costs sit outside both categories and are worth budgeting for separately:

  • Registered agent service - some states require a registered agent (a person or service that accepts legal documents on your LLC's behalf); if you don't act as your own, expect $50-$150/year on top of the state filing fee.
  • Business bank account and bookkeeping - not a hard requirement, but commingling personal and business funds undermines the liability protection an LLC is supposed to provide in the first place. A basic bookkeeping tool runs $0-$30/month.
  • Equipment replacement - resistance bands and mats wear out faster than most new trainers expect, especially with daily client use. Budget for a small ongoing replacement cost, not just the one-time purchase.
  • S-corp election - once your training income grows, electing S-corp tax treatment for your LLC can reduce self-employment tax, but adds payroll and accounting complexity most new trainers don't need in year one.

Do you actually need the LLC before you start?

No - you can legally train clients as a sole proprietor from day one. The trade-off is liability: without an LLC, a lawsuit against your business can reach your personal assets (savings, car, home equity), not just business assets. An LLC doesn't replace liability insurance - most trainers need both, not one instead of the other - but it adds a second layer of protection specifically around your personal finances. Many trainers start as a sole proprietor to keep initial costs low, then form an LLC once they have steady clients and something real to protect.

There's no universally "right" time to make the switch, but a reasonable trigger point is when you have enough regular clients that a single serious claim could realistically threaten savings you'd actually miss - at that point, the $224 first-year cost of an LLC is small relative to what it protects.

Budget note: a realistic minimal startup - certification plus a mobile equipment kit, before forming an LLC - can run under $1,000 total beyond insurance. Add $224+ if you form an LLC in year one, and more if you build toward a home studio.

FAQ

Do personal trainers need an LLC?

It's not legally required, but it's worth considering. Without an LLC, you're a sole proprietor - if a client sues over an injury, your personal assets are exposed, not just your business's. An LLC creates legal separation between you and the business. It's a decision to make alongside liability insurance, not a replacement for it.

How much does personal training equipment cost to get started?

It depends on your setup. A minimal mobile kit (resistance bands, a mat, a jump rope) runs $50-$100. A proper mobile strength kit (adjustable dumbbells, bands, a mat, a suspension trainer) runs $300-$600. A home studio with a rack, barbell, and flooring can run $1,000-$2,000 or more.

What's the cheapest way to start a personal training business?

Train mobile (at clients' homes or outdoors) with a minimal equipment kit, and operate as a sole proprietor without forming an LLC initially. This can get your total startup cost, beyond certification, down to a few hundred dollars - though you're trading lower upfront cost for the personal liability exposure of not having an LLC.
